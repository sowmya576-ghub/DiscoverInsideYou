# Voice and Tone

The narrator is an excellent science communicator speaking directly to one curious viewer.

## Tone

Curious · clear · warm · intelligent · wonder-filled · evidence-based ·
never condescending · never sensationalist.

Keep the same voice across episodes; let the emotion fit the topic (playful for
hiccups, calm and careful for immunity or aging).

## Language rules

- Short, spoken sentences. One idea at a time.
- Explain technical words immediately: "the arrector pili — a tiny muscle attached to each hair".
- Prefer concrete examples and analogies, and **say when an analogy is simplified**
  ("think of it — roughly — like…").
- Use "you" and "your body" naturally; don't overuse it.
- State uncertainty honestly: "scientists think", "one leading idea is", "we don't fully know yet".
- Match certainty words to the evidence level in `research.md`.

## Avoid

- Empty intros, greetings, "Hey guys, welcome back", "In today's video".
- Unsupported superlatives ("the most amazing organ"), fake urgency, fear-based hooks.
- Strings of rhetorical questions; one well-chosen question is enough.
- Withholding the answer just to hold retention.
- Robotic phrasing, overdramatic medical language, childish phrasing.
- Personal medical advice, diagnosis, treatment suggestions, promised health outcomes.

## Before / after

| Avoid | Prefer |
|-------|--------|
| "Hey guys! Today we're gonna look at something CRAZY your body does!" | "Every hair on your arm has its own tiny muscle." |
| "Your brain is the most powerful computer in the universe." | "Your brain builds what you see — and sometimes it guesses." |
| "This shocking fact will change how you see your body forever!" | "Here's what's actually happening under your skin." |

## Sign-off

"Discover Inside You — your body, your world, uncovered." Use selectively, when it closes
the story naturally. Not on every Short.

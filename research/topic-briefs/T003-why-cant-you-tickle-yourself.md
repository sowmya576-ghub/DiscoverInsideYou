## Audience question

Why does it tickle when someone else does it, but not when I try it myself?

## Current scientific answer (preliminary)

When you move, your brain predicts the touch that movement will cause, using a "forward
model" based on a copy of the motor command, and turns down its response. Self-produced
touch therefore feels weaker and less ticklish. fMRI work found less somatosensory activity
for self-produced touch and implicated the cerebellum in the prediction. Adding a delay or
changing the direction between your movement and the touch makes it more ticklish, as the
prediction account expects.

## Hook ideas (must be accurate)

- "Your brain knows your touch is coming — so it turns the volume down."
- Show a robot-arm setup: the longer the delay, the more it tickles (B1-S2, B1-S3).

## Uncertainty and caveats

- Cerebellar evidence comes from small, correlational fMRI studies (~1998–1999).
- Much classic work comes from one group; a 2023 preregistered study supports attenuation but didn't test tickle.
- Only abstracts were read for the 1998/1999 papers. Read the full texts before scripting.

## Misconceptions to address

- It's not that you "know where" the touch is; it's precise prediction *timing and direction* (delay experiments).
